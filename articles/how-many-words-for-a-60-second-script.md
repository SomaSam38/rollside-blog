# How many words for a 60 second script? Time it to your own pace

Ask how many words fit in a 60 second script and you will usually hear "about 150". That figure comes from a commonly quoted speaking rate of roughly 130 to 160 words per minute. It is a fair starting point. It is also an average, and averages are not much help when you are the one talking.

Some people speak quickly and clip their pauses. Others slow down for anything technical, or when reading from a prompter for the first time. A script that fits a minute for one creator can run well over for another. So the better question is: how many words do you say in a minute, with this kind of script?

## Measure your own speaking pace

This takes about two minutes and gives you a number you can keep using.

1. Take a script you have already written, or a paragraph from an old caption. Aim for 150 to 250 words.
2. Count the words. Most notes apps and word processors will do it for you.
3. Start a stopwatch and read it aloud at the pace you would use on camera. Not your rehearsal mumble, and not your nervous sprint.
4. Divide the word count by the seconds it took, then multiply by 60.

If 200 words took 85 seconds, your pace is 200 ÷ 85 × 60, or about 141 words per minute.

Do it twice, once with a casual script and once with something more detailed. If you slow down for steps, names and numbers, the second figure will be lower. Keep both.

## Turn your pace into a word budget

Once you know your pace, the maths is simple:

```text
words = your pace × seconds ÷ 60
```

Then take off a little for the parts of a video that are not words. A pause after your hook, a beat while you hold something up to the camera, a breath before the call to action. Taking off around 10 per cent is a reasonable buffer for short videos with a few deliberate pauses. If you plan to demonstrate something on screen, leave more.

For someone speaking at 140 words per minute, the budget looks like this:

- **30 seconds:** 70 words, so plan for about 60 to 65.
- **60 seconds:** 140 words, so plan for about 125.
- **90 seconds:** 210 words, so plan for about 190.
- **3 minutes:** 420 words, so plan for about 380.

These are targets for your first draft, not hard limits. The stopwatch makes the final call.

## A worked example: cutting a script to fit a minute

Say you are filming a 60 second Reel on how to clean a cast iron pan after cooking. Your pace is 140 words per minute, so your budget is around 125 words. Your first draft reads naturally but comes in at 182 words. That is roughly 78 seconds before any pauses.

Here is the opening paragraph of that draft:

> So a lot of people ask me about how to clean cast iron, and honestly I think there's a lot of confusion out there, so in this video I'm going to walk you through exactly what I do after I cook, step by step, so you never have to worry about rust again.

That is 53 words, and none of them teach anything yet. The hook is buried in the middle, and "so" appears three times. Here is the same opening after trimming:

> Cast iron pan after dinner? Here's how to clean it in four quick steps so it stays seasoned.

Now it is 18 words, and the promise comes first. That one cut saves 35 words, or about 15 seconds at this pace.

The rest of the trim tends to come from the same few places:

- **Throat clearing.** "In this video" and "so basically" fill time without moving anything forward.
- **Doubled steps.** If you say "rinse it with hot water" and then "make sure the water is hot", keep one.
- **Explanations the camera can show.** If viewers will see you wipe the pan dry, you do not need to describe the towel.
- **A long sign-off.** In a minute-long video, one clear next step is plenty.

After those edits, the example script lands at 128 words. At 140 words per minute that is about 55 seconds of speech, which leaves a few seconds for pauses. A timed read will tell you whether it really fits.

## Check the time by reading aloud, not by counting

Word counts are a planning tool. The real test is a timed read, standing or sitting the way you will film, at your filming pace.

Read it through twice. If both reads run long, cut more. If only one fits, you were probably rushing. Listen for where you sped up, because that is often a line too dense to say comfortably. Split it into two shorter sentences rather than talking faster.

Platform limits are worth checking before you record, because they change. If you are filming for a specific maximum length, aim a few seconds under it. That leaves room for the edit, a fade at the end or a line you deliver more slowly on the day.

## When a minute is the wrong length

Sometimes timing shows you something more useful than a word count: the idea does not fit. If you are cutting the steps that make the tip work, stop cutting and split it instead. "Cleaning cast iron after cooking" and "fixing rust on cast iron" are two separate videos, and each can be done well in a minute.
