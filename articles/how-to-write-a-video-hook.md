# How to write a video hook when it is just you talking to camera

"Hi everyone, welcome back, today I want to talk about something a lot of you have been asking about."

That line could open almost any video, which is the problem. When the whole video is you talking to camera, there is no b-roll or quick-cut montage to carry the first few seconds. Your face and your first sentence are the hook.

This guide will not hand you fifty templates. Instead, you write a handful of candidate openers for one video, say each out loud and keep the one that is easiest to deliver and clearest about what the viewer gets.

## Your hook has two jobs: the first frame and the first words

On many platforms, videos can start playing muted, and some people watch with the sound off. So think about what someone sees before you speak, as well as what they hear.

For the first frame:

- **Be ready when the video starts.** Cut any lead-in where you settle, look down or reach for the record button.
- **Show the subject if you can.** If the video is about an object, hold it in shot.
- **Put the hook on screen as text or captions** so a muted viewer gets the same promise as a listening one.

For the first words, the test is simpler. Does the sentence tell this viewer what they will get, in words they would use themselves?

## Write five openers before you write the rest

It is tempting to write the opening line first, then the script, and never revisit it. Try the reverse. Know roughly what the video covers, then write five different openers, each from a different angle:

1. **A question** the viewer is already asking.
2. **A plain promise** of what the video delivers.
3. **A mistake** the viewer might be making.
4. **A surprising detail** from inside the topic.
5. **A named viewer**, saying exactly who this is for.

Do not polish them yet. The point is to have real alternatives on the page, not one line you already feel attached to.

## A worked example: five openers for one pottery video

This example is hypothetical. Imagine a potter filming a short video about why mugs crack in the kiln. All five openers below were written for this article, not taken from a real channel, and nothing here is a claim about how viewers respond.

1. **Question:** "Have you ever opened the kiln and found a cracked mug?"
2. **Plain promise:** "Here's why your mugs crack in the kiln, and three things to change before your next firing."
3. **Mistake:** "That crack in your mug may have started days before it went in the kiln."
4. **Surprising detail:** "A crack across the base of a mug and a hairline crack up the side can have completely different causes, and the fix for one won't help with the other, so before you change your clay or your firing it's worth working out which one you've got."
5. **Named viewer:** "This one's for anyone who's lost a mug in the kiln."

Now read each one aloud, at the pace you would film.

Number 4 goes first. The idea is good, but it takes two breaths to say, and the viewer has to hold three clauses in their head before reaching the point. Spoken, it sounds like the middle of a video.

Number 5 goes next. It is friendly and easy to say, but it is vague. It names the viewer and then stops, so there is no reason to stay for the next sentence.

Number 1 is usable, but the honest answer is "yes", and a viewer who has answered your question does not need anything else from you. It names the problem without hinting at a fix.

That leaves 2 and 3. Number 2 is the clearest about the payoff. Number 3 is the more interesting line, because it reframes the problem: a viewer blaming the kiln is pointed somewhere else. It is also short enough to say in one easy breath.

The potter keeps number 3 as the hook. Number 2 is not thrown away, though. It becomes the second sentence.

## Score each opener by saying it, not reading it

The example used two checks you can apply to any opener:

- **One breath.** Say it at filming pace. If you need to breathe mid-line, or you stumble on the same word twice, simplify it.
- **Payoff named or clearly implied.** What would the viewer think they are about to learn? If you cannot answer in a few words, neither can they.

If two openers are still close, pick the one you would actually say to a friend. Lines that sound written ("In today's video, we explore") give themselves away once you hear them.

## Make the second sentence keep the promise

A hook earns the next few seconds, and the second sentence decides what you do with them. If the opener raises a question and the next line wanders into a long introduction, the viewer feels the gap.

In the pottery video, the finished opening reads:

> That crack in your mug may have started days before it went in the kiln. Here are three places cracks begin, and what to change at each one.

The first sentence creates the question. The second confirms the video will answer it and shows how it is organised. Then the script goes straight to the first of the three places. Introductions, channel plugs and "before we start" belong later, if anywhere.

Watch for openers that overpromise. If the hook says "the one mistake every potter makes" and the video lists five loosely related tips, the hook was a bait and switch, and viewers who stay will notice.

## Fit the hook to the rest of the script

Once the opening is settled, write the rest. If you are working to a tight length, the guide to [how many words fit in a 60 second script](/blog/how-many-words-for-a-60-second-script/) shows how to measure your own pace and trim to fit. For drafting the body in a voice that sounds like you, see [how to write a teleprompter script](/blog/how-to-write-a-teleprompter-script/).

Then rehearse the first line on its own until it sounds like speech rather than recital.
